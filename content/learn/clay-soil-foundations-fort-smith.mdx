## Quick answer

Fort Smith sits on expansive clay soil that swells when wet and shrinks when dry. When clogged gutters dump water at your foundation, that constant wet-dry cycle stresses the concrete — and foundation repairs commonly cost $10,000 or more. ## The 48-inch problem

Fort Smith averages about 48 inches of rain per year, according to the National Weather Service's long-term climate data for the area. That's roughly 10 inches more than the U.S. average. Every one of those inches lands on your roof first, and your roof funnels all of it into your gutters.

A 2,000-square-foot roof collects more than 1,200 gallons of water from just one inch of rain. Multiply that by 48 inches a year and you're looking at tens of thousands of gallons of water that your gutter system is responsible for moving away from your house. When that system works, it's invisible. When it fails, the water has exactly one place to go: straight down, into the soil touching your foundation.

## Why Fort Smith clay is different

Not all soil behaves the same way. The clay-heavy soils common around Fort Smith, Van Buren, and the Arkansas River Valley are "expansive," meaning they act like a sponge with an attitude:

- **When clay gets wet, it swells.** Waterlogged soil expands and pushes against your foundation walls and slab.
- **When clay dries out, it shrinks and pulls away.** The soil that was pressing against your foundation an hour ago can pull back and leave voids, letting the foundation settle unevenly.
- **The cycle is the enemy, not any single rainstorm.** A foundation that constantly shifts a little this way and a little that way develops cracks, sticking doors, sloping floors, and separation at windows and door frames.

This is why a "normal" rainy season here does more foundation damage than extreme weather does in other parts of the country. Our soil does the damage through repetition.

## How gutters connect to your foundation

Think of your gutter system as your home's water routing network. Its one job is to catch roof water and deliver it to a place where it can't hurt anything — ideally several feet away from the house, sloped away from the foundation.

Each failure point creates a water problem at the foundation line:

- **Clogged gutters overflow.** Water pours over the edge exactly where you don't want it: right against the house.
- **Short or missing downspout extensions** discharge a firehose of concentrated water at one spot along your foundation. That's the single wettest point in your whole drainage picture.
- **Sagging or badly pitched gutters** hold standing water, which overflows during heavy rain and adds weight that pulls the whole run loose — making the first two problems worse.
- **Underground drains that clog** back water up into the downspout until it spills over at the foundation.

None of these are exotic problems. On a typical Fort Smith home, at least one of them is happening right now.

## A five-minute self-check

Walk your house after the next good rain and look for three things:

1. **Water spilling over gutter edges** — a clog somewhere upstream.
2. **Downspouts discharging at the slab** — add extensions that carry water at least 4 feet out.
3. **Puddles that sit against the house for hours** — your grading or discharge point needs attention.
